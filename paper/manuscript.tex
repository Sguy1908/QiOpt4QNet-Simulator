\abstract{Routing in a quantum network has to account for entanglement fidelity, probabilistic generation and swapping, the cost of purification, finite memories and the chance that a delivered state misses its fidelity target. We treat concurrent entanglement requests as a selection problem: each request chooses at most one path-purification bundle, subject to edge and memory capacities. On physics-based synthetic networks we compare exact, heuristic, tensor-network and annealing solvers. CP-SAT, adaptive large-neighborhood search, parallel tempering and a tensor-network-initialized hybrid reach the reference utility in the exact and medium regimes, while a calibrated OpenJij simulated-quantum-annealing (SQA) ensemble stays 39 to 50\% below it. Screening bundles with a chance constraint lowers the out-of-sample fidelity-SLA violation rate from 16.3\% to 6.0\% and costs 6.9\% of optimization utility. For the QUBO form of the problem we derive one penalty coefficient per edge and per memory from the loads that other requests can actually place on that resource. With zero soft-congestion weights these coefficients keep the constrained optimum as the ground state. Over 1,080 generated instances they lower the mean edge and memory coefficients by 49.5\% and 46.3\%. In 32,340 matched runs on the 1,078 instances with at least one tighter coefficient, they raise the simulated-annealing (SA) feasible-read rate by 12.0 percentage points and cut its repaired reference gap by 10.8 points; for SQA the gains are 7.6 and 6.8 points. With better-configured samplers drawing 100 independent reads, the feasibility gain persists (8.7 and 8.0 points) but the repaired-gap gain shrinks to 1.0 and 1.9 points. The gain needs both the per-resource scope and the reachable-load bound: either one alone recovers at most two-fifths of the feasibility gain. Annealing remains well behind the classical solvers, and no quantum speedup is claimed.}

\keywords{quantum networks, entanglement routing, quantum repeaters, QUBO, tensor networks, chance constraints, stochastic optimization, resource allocation}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

A quantum internet would let remote nodes share high-fidelity entanglement for distributed computation, secure communication and networked sensing\cite{kimble2008,wehner2018,azuma2023}. Distributing that entanglement over long distances is hard. Optical loss suppresses elementary-link generation, local operations are imperfect and stored states decohere. Quantum repeaters address these limits by combining elementary generation, purification, storage and swapping\cite{briegel1998,dur1999,sangouard2011,azuma2023}. A network controller therefore has more to decide than a short path. It must choose which requests to admit, which paths they use, how much purification each link receives, how many raw Bell pairs and memories to reserve, and how much fidelity margin to keep when link quality fluctuates.

Work on entanglement routing has moved from path selection and capacity allocation towards formulations that track fidelity and resources explicitly. Pant \emph{et al.} showed that multipath structure can change remote-entanglement rates substantially\cite{pant2019}. Li \emph{et al.} combined candidate paths, finite edge capacities and purification in one routing design\cite{li2021}. Halder \emph{et al.} studied optimal routing and end-to-end distribution with exact optimization and scalable heuristics\cite{halder2024}, and later path-based purification models expose the fidelity-resource trade-off in online networks\cite{halder2026}. Xiao \emph{et al.} showed that purification should be scheduled jointly across concurrent flows\cite{xiao2024}. Quantum network utility maximization assigns value jointly to rate and entanglement quality\cite{vardoyan2023,kar2025}, and utility-optimal routing has been posed as a mixed-integer convex program\cite{kar2026}. A recent survey lists probabilistic swapping, finite memories, fidelity constraints, contention and control overhead as the main practical constraints on routing\cite{abane2025}.

Two gaps motivate this paper. The first concerns uncertainty. Many routing formulations optimize a deterministic surrogate, although generation probability, raw fidelity and memory quality vary between realizations. Chance-constrained programming and optimization over uncertainty sets separate nominal performance from reliability\cite{charnes1959,bertsimas2004}. The same separation fits quantum-network service-level agreements (SLAs): a route with high nominal fidelity can still miss a minimum-fidelity requirement after modest drift. Quality of service is now studied directly in quantum networking\cite{lopiparo2025}, but stochastic physical screening has not yet been tied to concurrent route and purification allocation.

The second gap concerns the QUBO encoding. Constrained allocation can be written as a quadratic unconstrained binary optimization (QUBO), which is the common input format for simulated annealing, quantum annealing and related samplers\cite{lucas2014,glover2019,kadowaki1998}. The penalty coefficients matter. If they are too small, infeasible states have low energy; if they are much larger than necessary, they compress the objective and make sampling harder\cite{verma2022,diez2022}. This is the QUBO version of the Big-M problem, which has recently been studied for quantum optimization\cite{alessandroni2025}. A single penalty set from the largest utility is always sufficient, but it ignores local structure. An edge that only low-value bundles use does not need the coefficient that an unrelated high-value bottleneck requires.

Tensor networks offer a third route. Matrix product states (MPS) and related networks can compress structured energy landscapes\cite{white1992,vidal2003,schollwock2011,orus2014}, and several groups have adapted them to constrained combinatorial optimization\cite{hao2022,nakada2025,preisser2026}. Rosales applied Hamiltonian and stochastic tensor-network methods to routing in large quantum-key-distribution (QKD) networks\cite{rosales2026}. Entanglement allocation differs from QKD flow routing: it must represent path-dependent swap fidelity, per-link purification, Bell-pair consumption, repeater memory and per-request fidelity targets. Whether tensor-network compression helps here has to be measured.

We present \emph{QiOpt4QNet}, a framework for allocating concurrent entanglement requests. For each request we generate candidate \emph{bundles}, each a path together with a purification depth on every link. Bundles are screened with loss-dependent generation, BBPSSW purification, imperfect swapping, storage during synchronization, Werner-state memory decay and random link perturbations. A binary allocation then picks at most one bundle per request within the edge Bell-pair and node-memory capacities (Fig.~\ref{fig:workflow}). We compare CP-SAT\cite{perron2023}, feasible greedy heuristics, parallel tempering\cite{hukushima1996}, adaptive large-neighborhood search (ALNS)\cite{ropke2006}, a stochastic boundary tensor-network solver, a hybrid that refines the tensor-network solution with ALNS, and OpenJij SA and SQA on the QUBO. SQA here is a classical simulation, not quantum hardware, and we make no claim of quantum advantage.

The paper makes four contributions.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item An allocation model over routes, purification and memory in which every solver receives the same screened candidate table, and every instance is exported with its physical parameters.
\item A regime-by-regime solver comparison. CP-SAT, ALNS, the hybrid and parallel tempering reach the reference on the exact and medium problems. Boundary tensor-network compression shows a width-quality-runtime trade-off, and a true-MPS ablation with fallback disabled shows that quality does not improve monotonically with bond dimension.
\item An out-of-sample test of nominal, risk-weighted and chance-constrained policies on a Monte Carlo stream that is independent of the one used to build them.
\item A per-resource QUBO calibration with a ground-state guarantee for zero soft-congestion weights. In 1,080 generated instances (1,078 evaluated, 32,340 SA and SQA runs) it improves SA feasibility and repaired quality substantially and SQA less. A check with better-configured samplers shows that the feasibility gain is robust while most of the repaired-quality gain is specific to a weak sampler, and ablations show that the gain needs both the per-resource scope and the reachable-load bound.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related work}\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Entanglement routing, purification and network utility}

A path in a quantum network is useful only if the network can create, store and transform entanglement of sufficient quality along it. Early repeater proposals set out the trade-offs between loss, memory and operation quality that make this so\cite{briegel1998,dur1999,sangouard2011,azuma2023}. At the network layer, Pant \emph{et al.} showed that generation can benefit from multipath structure instead of a single fixed chain\cite{pant2019}. Li \emph{et al.} combined several candidate paths, finite edge capacities and purification, and compared allocation strategies for simultaneous requests\cite{li2021}. Later formulations made fidelity guarantees and resource allocation explicit\cite{halder2024}, and the path-based purification model of Halder \emph{et al.} treats purification as part of online end-to-end allocation\cite{halder2026}.

Purification consumes several elementary pairs to raise fidelity, so a local operation draws on network-wide capacity. Xiao \emph{et al.} coordinate purification across concurrent requests to raise throughput under contention\cite{xiao2024}. Our bundle representation takes a different route to the same point. It precomputes per-edge purification depths for each path and passes the resulting edge and memory demands to the allocator. We do not reimplement their scheduler.

Network utility gives another view of allocation. Vardoyan and Wehner introduced quantum network utility maximization (QNUM), in which rate and quality jointly determine user utility\cite{vardoyan2023}. Kar and Wehner identified convexifiable cases\cite{kar2025}, and Kar and Mukhopadhyay extended the approach to route selection with a mixed-integer convex formulation and randomized rounding\cite{kar2026}. Lo Piparo \emph{et al.} examine quality of service in aggregated networks\cite{lopiparo2025}. QiOpt4QNet also allocates resources, but it discretizes each request into screened path-purification bundles. This makes heterogeneous purification and memory demand explicit, and every solver receives the same candidate table.

Recent surveys cover reactive, proactive, opportunistic and virtual-link routing and the growing role of swap probability, purification, memory lifetime and heterogeneous hardware\cite{abane2025}. Our physical screen is modular and analytic. It does not replace discrete-event simulators such as NetSquid\cite{coopmans2021} and SeQUeNCe\cite{wu2021}, which model hardware and protocols in detail; it gives a reproducible, controlled layer for optimization studies, and cross-validation against such simulators is future work. Stochastic automatic differentiation has also been used to optimize repeater designs when full simulations are noisy and expensive\cite{avis2025}.

\subsection{QUBO penalties and constrained annealing}

QUBO and Ising formulations turn constrained binary optimization into energy minimization and serve as a common input for simulated annealing, quantum annealing and special-purpose digital or analog optimizers\cite{lucas2014,glover2019,kirkpatrick1983,kadowaki1998}. Hard constraints become non-negative penalty terms whose coefficients must be large enough that no infeasible state is a ground state. Coefficients larger than that shrink the relative range of the objective and can hurt finite-precision or heuristic sampling.

Several papers address how to choose these coefficients. Verma and Lewis study penalty size and partitioning for QUBO solvers\cite{verma2022}, and Diez Garc{\'i}a \emph{et al.} derive exact and sequential penalty weights\cite{diez2022}. Alessandroni \emph{et al.} formalize the quantum Big-M problem and show why oversized constraint scales are costly when the Hamiltonian must fit a fixed energy range\cite{alessandroni2025}. Our contribution is narrower and exploits structure specific to this problem. Each request selects at most one bundle and each capacity is local, so the set of co-loads that can occur on a given resource is sparse. From that set we derive one sufficient coefficient per resource. We also test a global variant that uses the same derivation but keeps only the family-wide maximum. It tightens a coefficient in a minority of instances and changes solver behavior much less than the per-resource rule, which isolates the effect of coefficient scope from that of the derivation.

\subsection{Tensor networks and hybrid combinatorial search}

Tensor networks represent high-dimensional states at a cost set by their bond structure\cite{white1992,vidal2003,schollwock2011,orus2014}, and they are increasingly used for combinatorial optimization. Hao \emph{et al.} map locally constrained problems to a tensor-network construction\cite{hao2022}; Nakada \emph{et al.} build networks whose support contains only feasible configurations\cite{nakada2025}; Preisser \emph{et al.} combine variational product and MPS ans\"atze with iterated local search on large problems\cite{preisser2026}. Rosales applies Hamiltonian, Monte Carlo and stochastic boundary-MPS methods to QKD routing\cite{rosales2026}. None of these works claims that MPS compression will in general beat problem-specific classical solvers.

We designed the experiments with that in mind. Boundary-TN is scored before any refinement and across a sweep of retained widths. A separate SVD-based MPS solver runs with its greedy fallback disabled. The Boundary-TN+ALNS hybrid is reported as its own algorithm, since a good compressed starting point followed by exact neighborhood repair can work well even when the compressed solution alone does not.

\subsection{Reliability constraints in network control}

Chance-constrained programming goes back to Charnes and Cooper\cite{charnes1959}; Bertsimas and Sim quantified how much nominal objective is lost by protecting against uncertain data\cite{bertsimas2004}. Quantum networks fit this setting well. An allocation is useful only if the delivered states meet the application's fidelity requirement, and fidelity, generation time and operation success are all uncertain. Routing papers increasingly treat fidelity as a constraint\cite{li2021,halder2024,halder2026}, but most benchmarks still report deterministic or in-sample numbers. Our reliability experiment uses two sets of scenarios: one for scoring and screening candidates, and an independent Monte Carlo stream used only for evaluation. The resulting violation rate is an out-of-sample estimate under a stated noise model, not a device-level guarantee.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{A bundle model separates network control from physical screening}

Each request $r\in\Rset$ has a source, a destination, a priority $w_r$ and a minimum end-to-end fidelity $F_r^{\min}$. Instead of optimising path edges and purification rounds in one nonlinear model, QiOpt4QNet enumerates a finite bundle set $\Bset_r$ per request. A bundle $b\in\Bset_r$ fixes a path $p_b$ and a purification depth $q_{e,b}\in\{0,1,2\}$ on each of its edges, and carries its predicted fidelity and success probability, latency, edge demands $d_{eb}$, memory demands $m_{vb}$ and utility $u_b$ (nominal or risk-weighted). With $x_b\in\{0,1\}$ the allocation problem is
\begin{align}
\max_x\quad &\sum_b u_bx_b,\label{eq:allocobj}\\
\text{s.t.}\quad &\sum_{b\in\Bset_r}x_b\le 1 &&\forall r\in\Rset,\label{eq:req}\\
&\sum_b d_{eb}x_b\le C_e &&\forall e\in\Eset,\label{eq:edgecap}\\
&\sum_b m_{vb}x_b\le M_v &&\forall v\in\Vset.\label{eq:memcap}
\end{align}
Because the physics lives in the bundle table, the evaluator can be replaced without touching the optimizer, and every solver sees the same candidates and capacities.

The screening model is analytic and makes no claim to simulate specific hardware. Elementary generation probability falls with fiber attenuation and includes source, coupling and detector efficiencies. Purification follows the BBPSSW recurrence\cite{bennett1996}. One round on a Werner-like link\cite{werner1989} of fidelity $F$ succeeds with probability
\begin{equation}
 p_{\mathrm{pur}}(F)=F^2+\frac{2F(1-F)}{3}+5\left(\frac{1-F}{3}\right)^2,
\end{equation}
and on success the fidelity becomes
\begin{equation}
 F'=\frac{F^2+[(1-F)/3]^2}{p_{\mathrm{pur}}(F)}.
\end{equation}
Depth $q$ consumes $2^q$ raw pairs. Links are synchronized before swapping, so links that finish early wait in memory. For initial Werner-like fidelity $F_0$ the storage model is
\begin{equation}
F(t)=\frac14+\left(F_0-\frac14\right)\eta(t),\qquad
\eta(t)=e^{-t/T_2}\frac{1+e^{-t/T_1}}{2},\label{eq:memory}
\end{equation}
with $T_1=2T_2$ in the default benchmark. Equation~\ref{eq:memory} gives $F(0)=F_0$ and tends to the fully mixed value $1/4$. Two links are swapped with the Werner composition
\begin{equation}
F_{12}^{\mathrm{ideal}}=F_1F_2+\frac{(1-F_1)(1-F_2)}{3},
\end{equation}
followed by local depolarization with operation quality $\eta_s$, $F_{12}=\eta_sF_{12}^{\mathrm{ideal}}+(1-\eta_s)/4$. Path success is the product of the purified-link success probabilities and one swap-success factor per intermediate node.

We checked these formulas against an independent implementation before using them. The frozen physics validation drew 150,000 Monte Carlo samples, passed every memory invariant, and matched density-matrix calculations to $4.44\times10^{-16}$ for fidelity and $1.11\times10^{-16}$ for swapping. These checks confirm that the code implements the model; they do not calibrate the model to any platform. NetSquid\cite{coopmans2021} and SeQUeNCe\cite{wu2021} model hardware and control stacks in far more detail, and comparison against them remains to be done.

\subsection{Classical constrained optimizers set the reference}

We compared solvers on six regimes with 14 to 100 nodes and 12 to 80 requests (Table~\ref{tab:benchmark}). The exact regimes have 14 or 16 nodes and 12 or 14 requests, the medium regimes 32 or 40 nodes and 32 or 36 requests, and the large regimes 80 or 100 nodes and 72 or 80 requests. Exact and medium regimes use ten generated instances each and large regimes five. CP-SAT certified the optimum in the two exact regimes. Elsewhere the reference is the best feasible utility found by any solver, and the gap is labelled accordingly.

ALNS, the Boundary-TN+ALNS hybrid and parallel tempering matched the reference in every exact and medium regime and in the large-bottleneck regime (Fig.~\ref{fig:benchmark}). In the large-geometric regime ALNS and the hybrid defined the reference and parallel tempering missed it by $0.0003\%$. The fidelity-ordered greedy heuristic was close behind: its mean gap was $0\%$, $0.012\%$, $0.53\%$, $0.15\%$, $0.28\%$ and $0.29\%$ across the six regimes, at under 10 ms per instance. On these instances the screened candidate table already carries enough structure for a feasible heuristic to recover most of the available utility.

The stochastic boundary tensor-network solver (Boundary-TN) was competitive in some regimes and poor in others. Its mean gap was $5.89\%$ and $0\%$ in the two exact regimes, $3.54\%$ and $0.56\%$ in the medium regimes, $11.87\%$ on large-bottleneck and $0.60\%$ on large-geometric. The calibrated OpenJij SQA ensemble, run on the exact and medium regimes, stayed far from the reference, with mean gaps of $38.80\%$, $49.99\%$, $48.93\%$ and $47.03\%$ and median wall times of 2 to 4 hours per instance, three orders of magnitude slower than ALNS. Encoding the problem as a QUBO and sampling it does not, by itself, give competitive solutions.

A separate hardness sweep produced 768 solver-instance results over request counts 12 to 52, four capacity scales and eight seeds per cell. Mean gaps over the sweep were $1.07\%$ for Greedy-scarcity, $5.89\%$ for Boundary-TN, $0.035\%$ for ALNS and $0.022\%$ for the hybrid, with median runtimes of about $0.0014$, $4.88$, $4.20$ and $7.46$ s. Greedy quality fell as capacities tightened, but gradually: the worst cell mean was about $2.94\%$. A feasible greedy is enough for many instances, and the neighborhood search mainly trims the tail of poor allocations when resources are tightly coupled.

\subsection{Tensor-network compression trades quality for runtime}

To separate the tensor-network solver from any post-processing, we varied the retained Boundary-TN width $\chi$ with fallback disabled. The mean gap to ALNS fell from $13.19\%$ at $\chi=8$ to $13.13\%$, $9.11\%$, $8.13\%$, $7.79\%$ and $5.00\%$ at $\chi=16$, 32, 64, 128 and 256, while median runtime rose from $0.33$ s to $11.27$ s (Fig.~\ref{fig:tn}a). The mean improves steadily but individual instances do not: the gap decreased monotonically with $\chi$ for only four of the eight seeds. Keeping more boundary states captures more of the interaction structure, at a roughly linear cost in time.

We also ran an SVD-based MPS solver with greedy fallback disabled, to rule out a common benchmarking artifact in which a nominal tensor-network solver quietly returns a greedy solution. The ablation covers $\chi\in\{2,4,8,16,32,64\}$ and one, two or four sweeps on ten instances (180 runs). The fallback was never used. MPS quality was not monotonic in $\chi$ or in the number of sweeps (Fig.~\ref{fig:tn}b). Mean repaired gaps ranged from $11.6\%$ to $21.7\%$, against $0.17\%$ for the greedy heuristic on the same instances, and the MPS solution matched or beat greedy in only 12 of the 180 runs. For example, on seed 0 at $\chi=8$ with one sweep the MPS utility was 67.40 and the greedy utility 70.51. As a stand-alone solver the MPS is therefore weak on this problem; its performance depends on the variable ordering and the bond dimension, in line with recent MPS optimization studies\cite{preisser2026}.

\subsection{Reliability-aware selection trades utility for fewer SLA failures}

A bundle chosen because its nominal fidelity exceeds $F_r^{\min}$ can still fall short when raw fidelity or generation conditions drift. We compared three allocation policies on the same candidate physics. The \emph{nominal} policy maximizes the deterministic bundle utility. The \emph{risk-weighted} policy multiplies each bundle's mean scenario utility by its in-sample SLA probability and subtracts a fidelity-shortfall penalty. The \emph{chance-constrained} policy additionally removes bundles whose empirical SLA probability is below a threshold. The selected plans were then scored on a fresh Monte Carlo stream, independent of the scenarios used to build them.

Over 12 instances (36 runs), the nominal policy had the highest mean optimization utility, 124.25, and the highest out-of-sample violation rate, 16.3\% (Fig.~\ref{fig:reliability}). The risk-weighted policy lowered violations to 12.1\% at utility 114.51. The chance-constrained policy lowered them to 6.0\%, with utility 115.64 and slightly higher delivered fidelity (0.863 against 0.860). Compared with the nominal policy, chance-constrained selection cut violations by about 63\% and gave up 6.9\% of utility. The ordering holds instance by instance: the chance-constrained policy had fewer violations than the nominal one on all 12 instances.

The chance constraint is estimated from samples under the stated noise model, so it does not prove a reliability guarantee for real hardware. Keeping the construction scenarios separate from the evaluation stream does remove the most obvious source of in-sample optimism, and it shows the trade-off a network operator would face when choosing an acceptable violation rate.

\subsection{Per-resource QUBO penalties are about half the conventional scale}

For the annealing baselines, the constraints \eqref{eq:req}--\eqref{eq:memcap} are folded into an unconstrained energy (Methods). Let $P_0=\max_b u_b^+$ be the largest positive bundle utility. The conventional rule sets the request-conflict, edge and memory penalties to $A=B=D=P_0+\eps$. This guarantees that no single bundle's utility can outweigh the smallest unit of constraint violation, but it gives every capacity constraint the scale set by the most valuable bundle in the instance.

The per-resource rule (Methods, Eq.~\ref{eq:resourceB}) instead sets each edge and memory coefficient from the loads that other requests can actually place on that resource. We checked its ground-state guarantee (Proposition~\ref{prop:ground}) by exhaustive enumeration: in 2,000 randomized small instances, every QUBO ground state was feasible and optimal, covering request conflicts as well as edge and memory overloads.

The calibration experiment generated 1,080 instances from four chain and grid configurations, 90 generation seeds and 8, 16 or 24 requests. In 1,078 of them the per-resource rule made at least one coefficient strictly tighter than the conventional one. These 1,078 instances were solved with conventional, global and per-resource calibration, using SA and SQA, five solver seeds and 100 requested reads, giving 32,340 solver runs. Selection depended only on the coefficients, never on solver results; in the two excluded instances no rule changes any coefficient, so they carry no information about the treatment. Solver seeds were averaged within each instance before inference, and because each generation seed recurs across configurations, confidence intervals resample the 90 generation-seed blocks.

Per-resource calibration roughly halved the penalty scale (Fig.~\ref{fig:coeff}). Averaged over all 1,080 instances it lowered the mean edge and memory coefficients by 49.5\% and 46.3\% relative to the conventional value; the global rule lowered them by only 4.3\% and 3.0\%. It tightened 88.7\% of edge and 80.3\% of memory coefficients. These figures average over each instance's resources, on average 12.4 edges and 10.4 memories, of which 11.0 and 8.3 were tightened. They therefore describe the \emph{non-binding} penalties. The largest per-resource coefficient in an instance always equals the global one, because $B_{\mathrm{global}}=\max_eB_e$ by construction. Tightening was largest at low load. The mean per-resource edge and memory ratios to the conventional coefficient were $0.352$ and $0.350$ at 8 requests, $0.531$ and $0.575$ at 16 and $0.631$ and $0.686$ at 24; the global ratios were $0.889$ and $0.914$, $0.986$ and $0.996$, and $0.997$ and $1.000$. With more requests the reachable co-load sets fill in, small penalty drops become more common, and there is less room below the conventional scale.

\subsection{With better-configured samplers the feasibility gain persists and the quality gain shrinks}

The OpenJij SA schedule used above leaves SA far from the reference. With independent reads it still has a mean repaired gap of about 30 to 60\%, at 10 or 100 reads and with ten times the sweeps (Supplementary Information). Large effects measured on such a weak sampler could reflect the sampler rather than the encoding. We therefore repeated the comparison on all 1,078 instances with samplers that explore more: SA from dwave-samplers with 1,000 sweeps, and OpenJij SQA with the settings above, each drawing 100 independent reads per run with one solver seed (Methods). Two ablations were added, both of which keep the ground-state guarantee. The \emph{local-utility} rule keeps one coefficient per resource but sets it to the largest positive utility of any bundle using that resource, without the reachable-load bound. \emph{Per-pair} request penalties replace the uniform $A$ by $A_{bc}=\min(u_b^+,u_c^+)+\eps$ on top of the per-resource rule.

Under these samplers the absolute gaps are small: conventional calibration leaves a mean repaired gap of 6.21\% for SA and 5.01\% for SQA, against 86.30\% and 29.69\% in the main study (Table~\ref{tab:calib-sampler}). The per-read feasibility gain of per-resource calibration persists: $+8.67$ pp for SA (CI $[7.85,9.51]$) and $+7.99$ pp for SQA ($[7.15,8.87]$), with per-resource calibration better on 77\% of instances for both samplers. The repaired-gap gain is far smaller than in the main study: $-1.01$ pp for SA ($[-1.36,-0.68]$) and $-1.87$ pp for SQA ($[-2.13,-1.63]$), with per-resource calibration worse on 32.3\% and 17.3\% of instances. With 100 distinct samples the best repaired read is already close to the reference under conventional calibration, so there is little gap left to close. The quality effects of the main study should therefore be read as effects on a weak, effectively single-sample annealer, and the feasibility effect as the more general finding.

The ablations separate the two ingredients of the rule. The global rule again changes little ($+0.85$ and $+0.97$ pp in feasibility for SA and SQA). The local-utility rule, which keeps the per-resource scope but drops the reachable-load bound, gives $+3.55$ and $+3.20$ pp, about two-fifths of the per-resource gain. Adding the bound raises feasibility by a further $5.11$ pp for SA (CI $[4.48,5.75]$) and $4.79$ pp for SQA ($[4.12,5.46]$). For repaired gap the samplers differ: for SA the local-utility rule matches the per-resource rule ($-0.07$ pp, CI $[-0.30,0.16]$), while for SQA the bound lowers the gap by a further $0.64$ pp ($[-0.83,-0.45]$). Neither ingredient is sufficient for the feasibility gain on its own. The earlier inference that scope accounts for nearly all of the gain held only against the global rule, which combines the bound with a family-wide maximum.

Per-pair request penalties trade raw feasibility for repaired quality. They lower the conflict penalty between low-utility bundles, so samples select several bundles of one request more often: mean conflicts per read rise by 1.00 (SA) and 1.26 (SQA), and the raw feasible rate falls by 26.2 and 20.7 pp. Repair drops the extra bundles, and the repaired gap falls by a further 0.72 and 0.96 pp. Raw overload also behaves differently under these samplers: per-resource calibration slightly raises SA overload ($+0.031$ units, CI $[0.023,0.040]$) and lowers SQA overload ($-0.65$, $[-0.69,-0.61]$), the reverse of the pattern in the main study. This check uses one solver seed, and its 36 comparisons are again reported without multiplicity correction.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

On the problems tested here, what separates good from poor results is how thoroughly the allocation is searched. A feasible greedy allocation is already near-optimal in most exact and medium instances, and CP-SAT, ALNS, the TN-initialized hybrid and parallel tempering close almost all of the remaining gap. The cost of global search pays off mainly on instances with tightly coupled resource conflicts. The 768-run hardness sweep agrees: greedy quality drops as capacities tighten, but gradually and unevenly across instances.

Tensor networks are best treated here as a compression tool. Boundary-TN shows a clear average trade-off between width and quality, while the true-MPS results show that a larger bond dimension or more sweeps need not help. This matches the wider tensor-network optimization literature, where encoding, variable ordering, feasibility structure and the surrounding local search matter as much as the choice of network\cite{hao2022,nakada2025,preisser2026}. A hybrid design, in which compressed global structure supplies a starting point and exact local repair improves it, is consistent with our Boundary-TN+ALNS results, although the benchmarks do not show a general runtime advantage.

Reliability should be an explicit objective. Routing work already includes fidelity and purification\cite{li2021,xiao2024,halder2024,halder2026}, and utility formulations assign value to both rate and quality\cite{vardoyan2023,kar2025,kar2026}. Our out-of-sample test adds one observation: optimising nominal utility alone understates the risk of missing fidelity targets. Chance-constrained screening more than halves the violation rate under our noise model, at a cost in utility. Where to sit on that trade-off is a policy decision, and the framework reports the trade-off instead of fixing a risk tolerance.

Penalty calibration deserves attention of its own. Generic QUBO practice often uses one objective-scale penalty because it is simple and always sufficient\cite{glover2019,verma2022}, and recent work on the quantum Big-M problem shows why oversized coefficients hurt quantum and quantum-inspired optimizers\cite{alessandroni2025}. Our results add a mechanism specific to allocation problems. When each request selects at most one bundle, only certain co-loads are reachable, so the smallest real overload step can exceed the unit step that a global bound assumes. A dynamic program over requests finds these steps. The coefficient must then stay local to each edge or memory; taking the maximum restores the global scale. This explains both the roughly twofold reduction in mean coefficients and why the global rule, which changes a coefficient in only 127 of 1,078 instances, has a small pooled effect. The ablation adds that the bound matters once the coefficient is local: a per-resource coefficient set from bundle utilities alone recovers only about two-fifths of the feasibility gain.

The physical model sets limits on these conclusions. It is an analytic screen built on Werner-state fidelities, BBPSSW purification, independent random link perturbations and a phenomenological $T_1/T_2$ memory envelope. It leaves out multiplexing control overheads, correlated noise, finite classical-control queues, detector dark counts and protocol-specific memory scheduling. The internal physics checks test the implementation, not the model against NetSquid, SeQUeNCe or experiment. The benchmark topologies are synthetic, and the evidence does not show that the results generalize across topologies: a topology-generalization run was incomplete at the freeze and is excluded, as is an incomplete extension following Rosales. OpenJij SQA is a classical simulator; the study includes no quantum-annealing hardware and makes no speedup claim.

The calibration study has further limits. The ground-state guarantee holds for $C=E=0$, the setting used in every experiment; with positive soft-congestion weights the ground state need not be optimal. The study uses four small chain and grid configurations with 8 to 16 nodes and 8 to 24 requests, so its generality across topologies is untested. Fixed-seed reads are identical, so in the main study solver variability comes only from the five seeds and extra reads change nothing. With independent reads but the same OpenJij SA schedule, the per-resource advantage did not shrink with budget: $-13.2$ pp in repaired gap at 10 reads, $-13.1$ pp at 100 reads and $-15.8$ pp with ten times the sweeps (Supplementary Information). With a better-configured SA it fell to $-1.0$ pp. The size of the quality effect therefore depends on the sampler, while the feasibility effect stayed between $+7.6$ and $+13.2$ pp across all three protocols. The local-utility ablation removes the reachable-load bound and redundant-constraint detection together, so their separate contributions remain unknown, and the sampler check uses a single solver seed. Per-resource calibration worsened the repaired gap on 24 to 26\% of instances in the main study and on 17 to 32\% in the sampler check, and it raised raw overload for one of the two samplers in each protocol. Comparisons with other penalty-setting methods, such as the exact and sequential weights of Ref.~\cite{diez2022}, and larger solver-seed sets remain to be done.

These limits point to specific next steps. The most useful would be to replay a representative set of selected bundles in a full-stack simulator and later on a hardware-informed testbed. Correlated link and memory noise could replace the independent scenario model, and larger networks could be studied with explicit distributed-control costs. The per-resource calibration is not specific to quantum networks. It applies to any multiple-choice, capacity-constrained QUBO for which request-conditioned reachable loads can be computed efficiently, and testing it on multidimensional knapsack and related allocation problems is a natural follow-up.

In summary, fidelity, purification, memory and reliability determine which network actions are physically sound, and classical constrained optimization sets a demanding reference against which tensor-network and annealing methods should be measured. On the instances studied, simple allocation is often sufficient, global and hybrid search repair tightly coupled cases, chance constraints buy reliability at a known cost, and per-resource QUBO penalties raise the feasibility of annealing samples without changing the problem being solved.

\section{Methods}\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Network, requests and candidate paths}

A network is an undirected graph $G=(\Vset,\Eset)$. Each link has a length, raw fidelity, elementary generation probability and Bell-pair capacity per epoch; each node has a memory capacity. For each request we generate up to $k$ simple paths and expand each into purification profiles with at most two BBPSSW rounds per link. Profiles that are dominated, physically invalid or fail the fidelity or SLA screen are removed. The benchmark regimes use $k=5$ or 6 and keep at most 16 to 18 bundles per request to limit combinatorial growth. All instances and their physical parameters are seeded and exported.

Elementary link generation uses
\begin{equation}
p_{\mathrm{gen}}(L)=\mathrm{clip}\!\left(\eta_s\eta_c\eta_d10^{-\alpha L/10}\,\xi,\,0.03,\,0.995\right),
\end{equation}
where $\alpha=0.20$ dB km$^{-1}$, $(\eta_s,\eta_c,\eta_d)=(0.98,0.97,0.95)$ and $\xi$ is a per-link factor. The expected time to prepare $n$ raw pairs with $m$ parallel modes is $n/(p_{\mathrm{gen}}mf_{\mathrm{attempt}})$ with $f_{\mathrm{attempt}}=2\times10^5$ Hz. The synchronization time is the longest preparation time along the path, and links that finish earlier decohere according to Eq.~\ref{eq:memory}. Classical signalling adds $4.9\,\mu$s per kilometer and each swap $5\,\mu$s.

For a nominally evaluated bundle the service term is
\begin{equation}
v_b=w_r p_b\left[1+\max(F_b-F_r^{\min},0)\right],
\end{equation}
and the utility is
\begin{equation}
u_b=v_b-\lambda_L L_b-\lambda_B N_b,
\end{equation}
with latency penalty $\lambda_L=0.0015$ and raw-pair penalty $\lambda_B=0.025$. This utility is a benchmark objective, not an economic value of entanglement. It rewards success probability and fidelity margin and charges for latency and pair consumption.

\subsection{Scenario sampling and chance screening}

To build the risk-weighted and chance-constrained candidates, raw fidelities receive independent Gaussian perturbations with standard deviation 0.008, and generation probabilities are multiplied by lognormal factors centered near one with log standard deviation 0.10. For scenarios $s=1,\ldots,S$ the evaluator returns $F_b^{(s)}$, $p_b^{(s)}$ and utility $u_b^{(s)}$. We compute the empirical SLA probability
\begin{equation}
\hat q_b=\frac{1}{S}\sum_{s=1}^S\mathbf{1}\{F_b^{(s)}\ge F_r^{\min}\},
\end{equation}
the mean utility $\bar u_b$ and the mean fidelity shortfall $\bar\delta_b=\frac1S\sum_s\max(F_r^{\min}-F_b^{(s)},0)$. The risk-weighted utility is
\begin{equation}
u_b^{\mathrm{rw}}=\bar u_b\hat q_b-\lambda_S w_r\bar\delta_b,
\end{equation}
with $\lambda_S=0.65$. The chance-constrained policy also requires $\hat q_b\ge0.80$. The reliability study uses 12 instances; for each, the selected bundles are evaluated on 400 fresh Monte Carlo draws generated independently of the construction scenarios.

\subsection{Constrained solvers}

CP-SAT provides the exact reference where it can certify optimality. The greedy baselines sort bundles by a scarcity-aware or fidelity-aware score and admit a bundle only if the request, edge and memory constraints stay satisfied. Parallel tempering runs replicas at several temperatures with local selection moves and replica exchange\cite{hukushima1996}. ALNS repeatedly frees a subset of request assignments and re-solves that neighborhood with CP-SAT, adapting the neighborhood choice to the incumbent. The hybrid starts from a Boundary-TN solution and applies the same neighborhood search. Each solver's result is reported as produced; no solver silently falls back to another, and the hybrid is named as such.

The Boundary-TN solver represents the binary interactions created by request conflicts and shared resources, processes variables in a conflict-aware order, and keeps at most $\chi$ boundary states using log-domain weights and diversity-preserving compression. It enforces feasibility while branching instead of penalizing infeasible states afterwards. A separate MPS solver performs SVD sweeps and reports raw MPS, repaired MPS and greedy utilities separately, with greedy fallback disabled in the ablation.

\subsection{QUBO encoding}

For the annealing baselines each bundle is one binary variable and the utility enters as $-\sum_bu_bx_b$. Request conflicts use the slack-free pairwise term
\begin{equation}
H_{\mathrm{req}}=A\sum_{r\in\Rset}\sum_{b<c\in\Bset_r}x_bx_c.
\end{equation}
Capacity inequalities become squared equality penalties with binary slack variables:
\begin{align}
H_{\mathrm{edge}}&=B\sum_{e\in\Eset}\left(\sum_bd_{eb}x_b+s_e-C_e\right)^2,\\
H_{\mathrm{mem}}&=D\sum_{v\in\Vset}\left(\sum_bm_{vb}x_b+t_v-M_v\right)^2.
\end{align}
Optional quadratic congestion terms, with weights $C$ and $E$, are kept separate from the hard-constraint penalties and set to zero in the calibration study; the ground-state result below is proved only for that case. In these expressions $B$ and $D$ are single coefficients: the conventional baseline uses the utility scale and the global control uses the family-wide calibrated values. The per-resource form follows.

\subsection{Reachable-load calibration}
Consider resource $j$ with capacity $C_j$ and a candidate bundle $b$ with demand $d_{jb}$. Since each request selects at most one bundle, let $\mathcal{L}_{j,-r(b)}$ be the set of co-loads on $j$ reachable by selecting zero or one bundle from every request other than $r(b)$. For co-load $\ell$, removing $b$ from a violated squared-overload penalty lowers that penalty by
\begin{equation}
\Delta_{j,b}(\ell)= [\max(0,\ell+d_{jb}-C_j)]^2-[\max(0,\ell-C_j)]^2.\label{eq:delta}
\end{equation}
Among reachable co-loads with $\ell+d_{jb}>C_j$, the smallest gives the smallest useful penalty drop for this squared-overload form. We therefore set
\begin{equation}
B_j=\max_{b:\,d_{jb}>0}\frac{u_b^+}{\Delta_{j,b}}+\eps,\label{eq:resourceB}
\end{equation}
with $\Delta_{j,b}$ evaluated at that smallest reachable violating co-load. Edge coefficients are $B_e$ and memory coefficients $D_v$, defined the same way. The request-conflict coefficient stays at $A=P_0+\eps$. The global control takes the maximum of these bounds over all edges, $B_{\mathrm{global}}=\max_e B_e$, and likewise over memories; the \emph{per-resource} rule keeps them separate.

\begin{proposition}[Ground-state sufficiency]\label{prop:ground}
Assume non-negative integer demands and capacities, at most one bundle per request, zero soft-congestion weights ($C=E=0$), and coefficients given by Eq.~\ref{eq:resourceB} with $\eps>0$. Then every global minimum of the QUBO energy satisfies the edge and memory capacity constraints, and, with $A>P_0$, contains no request conflict. The ground state therefore attains the constrained optimal utility.
\end{proposition}
\noindent\textit{Proof sketch.} For a fixed bundle assignment the slack variables can represent every integer in $[0,C_j]$, so minimizing over them turns each capacity term into $B_j[\max(0,L_j-C_j)]^2$ for load $L_j$; it suffices to compare these slack-minimized energies. First, a state in which some request selects two or more bundles is not a global minimum: dropping one of them removes at least one pairwise conflict term, worth $A>P_0\ge u_b^+$, and cannot raise any capacity penalty because demands are non-negative. So every global minimum selects at most one bundle per request, and the co-load of any selected bundle $b$ on resource $j$ is then reachable in the sense of $\mathcal{L}_{j,-r(b)}$. Second, suppose such a state overloads $j$, and pick a selected $b$ with $d_{jb}>0$. Its co-load $\ell$ is reachable and violating, so $\ell$ is at least the smallest reachable violating co-load, and because $\Delta_{j,b}(\ell)$ is non-decreasing in $\ell$ once $\ell+d_{jb}>C_j$, removing $b$ lowers the penalty on $j$ by at least $B_j\Delta_{j,b}>u_b^+$ while losing at most $u_b^+$ of objective and raising no other penalty. The state therefore has a strictly lower-energy neighbour. Every global minimum is feasible, and among feasible states the energy equals minus the utility. $\square$

\begin{remark}
The condition $C=E=0$ is needed for the last statement. Soft congestion terms penalize feasible loads, so with positive weights the ground state need not be utility-optimal, and we claim no optimality guarantee in that case. All calibration experiments use $C=E=0$.
\end{remark}

For each resource and candidate bundle, a bounded dynamic program builds the loads reachable by choosing at most one demand option from each other request. Only loads near the first capacity violation matter. The smallest reachable load that overflows after adding a candidate of demand $d$ is at most $C_j-d+d_{\max}$, where $d_{\max}$ is the largest single demand among the other requests, so no load above $C_j+d_{\max}$ is needed. The truncated set therefore does not depend on the candidate and can be cached per excluded request. The smallest reachable violating co-load gives Eq.~\ref{eq:delta}, and the coefficient is the largest ratio of positive utility to penalty drop over candidate bundles, plus $\eps=\max(10^{-9},10^{-6}P_0)$. Resources that can never be overloaded receive only $\eps$. The result is one $B_e$ per edge and one $D_v$ per memory, and the global control replaces them by their family-wide maxima. The per-resource penalties are
\begin{align}
H_{\mathrm{edge}}&=\sum_{e\in\Eset}B_e\left(\sum_bd_{eb}x_b+s_e-C_e\right)^2,\\
H_{\mathrm{mem}}&=\sum_{v\in\Vset}D_v\left(\sum_bm_{vb}x_b+t_v-M_v\right)^2.
\end{align}

\subsection{Calibration instances and sampler settings}

The calibration instances are generated by the bundle model of the QiOpt4QNet repository, which is simpler than the screening model used for the benchmark and reliability studies. Every link of a configuration has the same raw fidelity and generation probability ($0.85$ and $0.95$ for the 8-node chain and the $3\times3$ grid, $0.88$ and $0.90$ for the 10-node chain and the $4\times4$ grid), a length of 25 km and a latency of 5 units. Each request receives $k=3$ candidate paths, and each path is expanded with one purification depth $q\in\{0,1,2\}$ applied to all of its links. A link at depth $q$ demands $2^q$ Bell pairs; its success probability is $p_{\mathrm{gen}}^{2^q}$ times the BBPSSW success probabilities of the rounds, end-to-end fidelity follows the Werner swap composition without operation noise, and there is no storage decoherence. The utility is $w_rp_b[1+\max(F_b-F_r^{\min},0)]-0.01L_b-0.05N_b$. The coefficient rules use only the bundle table, so this choice affects the calibration study only through the instances it produces.

OpenJij samplers run with their defaults: SA with 1000 sweeps and a $\beta$ range set automatically from the coefficients of each QUBO, and SQA with $\beta=5$, $\Gamma=1$, 1000 sweeps and four Trotter slices. Because the SQA inverse temperature is fixed in absolute units, rescaling the penalty coefficients also changes its effective temperature. Repair is drop-only: it keeps the highest-utility selected bundle of each request, admits the kept bundles in decreasing order of utility while they fit, and never adds a bundle the sampler did not select. The repaired gap therefore measures the content of the sampled state, not the quality of a repair heuristic.

\subsection{Calibration experiment and statistics}

The calibration study uses four topology-capacity configurations: an 8-node chain with edge capacity 4, a $3\times3$ grid with capacity 4, a 10-node chain with capacity 6 and a $4\times4$ grid with capacity 5. With 90 generation seeds and request counts 8, 16 and 24 this gives 1,080 instances, none of them empty. An instance entered the solver study if the global or the per-resource family-mean coefficient ratio to the conventional coefficient was below $0.999999$. This criterion depends only on the coefficients and selected 1,078 instances. Each was solved under three calibrations (conventional, global and per-resource), with two samplers (OpenJij SA and SQA) and five fixed solver seeds ($101,202,303,404,505$), requesting 100 reads per run: $1078\times3\times2\times5=32{,}340$ solver runs. CP-SAT certified the reference utility.

With a fixed seed, OpenJij returns identical reads within one call for both samplers. The number of samples equals the number requested, but only one is distinct; we verified this for 1, 100 and 1,000 requested reads. Each call therefore contributes one sample per solver seed, and every archived call has a raw feasible rate of 0 or 1. A separate budget-sensitivity runner draws every read as its own seeded call, which makes extra reads independent at considerable computational cost.

The statistical unit is the generated instance. For each instance, sampler and calibration, the five solver seeds are averaged before inference. The same generation seed appears in several configurations, so 95\% confidence intervals are percentile intervals from 10,000 bootstrap resamples of the 90 generation-seed blocks, keeping all instances within a sampled block. The statistic is the ratio of sums, which equals the overall mean paired difference, and each comparison's resampling seed is derived from its identity so that the intervals are reproducible. The outcomes are raw feasible-read rate, repaired gap to the CP-SAT reference and raw overload, for SA and SQA, for three contrasts (global against conventional, per-resource against conventional and per-resource against global), plus the global-against-conventional contrast restricted to the 127 instances in which the global rule changes a coefficient: $3\times2\times3+1\times2\times3=24$ comparisons. No multiplicity correction is applied. The intervals describe variation across generated instances with five fixed solver seeds, not variation across solver seeds.

\subsection{Sampler-configuration check}

The sampler check reuses the 1,078 selected instances and their CP-SAT references. SA is the \texttt{SimulatedAnnealingSampler} of dwave-samplers with 1,000 sweeps and its default $\beta$ range, drawing 100 reads in one call; its reads are independent within a call. SQA uses the OpenJij settings above, with each of the 100 reads drawn as its own call with seeds $101,102,\ldots$. Each instance is solved once per sampler and calibration, with solver seed 101, under five calibrations: conventional, global, per-resource, local-utility and per-resource with per-pair request penalties. The local-utility rule sets $B_e=\max u_b^+ +\eps$ over the bundles that use edge $e$, and likewise $D_v$; it is sufficient because with integer demands removing a bundle from an overloaded resource lowers that resource's squared overload by at least one. The per-pair rule sets $A_{bc}=\min(u_b^+,u_c^+)+\eps$ for every pair of bundles of one request; it is sufficient because dropping the lower-utility bundle $c$ of a conflicting pair loses at most $u_c^+<A_{bc}$ and cannot raise a capacity penalty. Outcomes, drop-only repair and the generation-seed-block bootstrap are as in the main study, with one run per instance in place of the five-seed average, giving $1078\times5\times2=10{,}780$ solver runs. The 36 comparisons in Table~\ref{tab:calib-sampler} (6 contrasts, 2 samplers, 3 outcomes) are not corrected for multiplicity.

\subsection{Benchmark and tensor-network experiments}

The benchmark has six regimes. Exact and medium regimes use ten seeds and large regimes five. CP-SAT is run when the request count allows certification; otherwise the reference is the best feasible utility among the compared methods. The hardness sweep crosses request counts $\{12,20,28,36,44,52\}$ with capacity scales $\{1.10,0.90,0.75,0.62\}$ and eight seeds.
